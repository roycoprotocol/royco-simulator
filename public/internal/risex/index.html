What a tranche is, in four lines.

  • One vault, two claims on it. The Senior takes a protected, predictable rate. The Junior takes the first losses and is paid more for it.
  • The Junior is the buffer. When the vault falls, the Junior absorbs it. The Senior only loses once that buffer is used up.
  • Why a protocol wants this: the Senior is a product you can sell to capital that will not take drawdown risk, which is most capital. The Junior is sold to capital that will.
  • The only real question is how deep the buffer has to be. That is what this page is for.

  • Why the Senior rate never moves. Every dollar of Senior capital hands the Junior the same slice of its own yield, whatever the cushion. So the Senior always keeps the rest, and its rate is flat all the way across.
  • Why the Junior rate climbs as the cushion thins. Fewer Junior dollars share that same slice. At a 5% cushion each Junior dollar stands behind of Senior capital; at 50% it stands behind . Same transfer, much smaller base.
  • The cushion is the minimum coverage, and the Junior is sized a little above it. Coverage is what the Junior must be able to absorb of Senior losses at all times. Royco's markets hold a slight excess of Junior capital over that minimum, targeting 90% utilization, so Juniors can keep entering and exiting without instantly breaching it. A 20% minimum coverage means a Junior worth 22.2% of the market.
  • The formulas. Senior APR = vault yield x (1 minus yield share). Junior APR = vault yield x (1 plus yield share x Senior size / Junior size), with Junior size = cushion / 90% target utilization. Simple annual rates, not compounded.
  • What these are not. Contractual splits of whatever the vault earns, not returns anyone expects it to deliver. Section 2 shows what the split did on the real record.
